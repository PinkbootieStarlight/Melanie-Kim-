You picked a good one. Physician integration is a live, consequential question — it is reshaping
who employs doctors across the country, and it sits right on the seam this course cares about, where
the efficiency story and the market-power story pull against each other. Your hypothesis already
holds both: "Integration may generate genuine economies of scale and administrative efficiencies,
but increased concentration and provider bargaining power may contribute to higher healthcare
prices. The analysis will determine whether measurable efficiencies are sufficient to offset those
effects." That is a hypothesis that can lose, which is the first thing I look for and the thing many
briefs never manage. And your fourth falsification condition — that quality gains might offset price
increases — is the one most people writing on this topic forget entirely. You wrote it down before
touching the data. Good.

What the brief needs now is to become measurable, so the weighing you promise can actually happen
on the page. Three moves get you there, and the first one carries the other two.

**Give the paper one relationship with a number at each end.** Right now the planned analysis is a
literature synthesis — five bullets that begin "examine" — and a four-page synthesis of this
literature will read as a report on what others found rather than an argument of yours. Your
hypothesis has already chosen the relationship: the share of physicians in employed or
hospital-owned practice, against the price of physician services. One trend, one outcome. The AMA's
physician practice benchmark surveys are one place to start for the employment share by year; for
the price side, look for a price series or a price differential between integrated and independent
practices in the hospital–physician integration literature. Name your two sources in the brief, and the rest of the literature turns into support for the mechanism instead of the paper's
spine. This is also your figure: employment share and price on the same time axis. If the two series
will not line up cleanly, a diagram of the two opposing forces is a perfectly respectable fallback.

**Let your hypothesis choose the concepts.** The micro paragraph lists eight — economies of scale,
access to capital, transaction costs, bargaining power, market concentration, barriers to entry,
referral incentives, competition. Your hypothesis uses two: scale economies on the efficiency side,
bargaining power and concentration on the price side. Keep those and give each of the others a
sentence at most. The paper gets sharper, not thinner.

**Decide who acts on your answer.** Recommendation is the heaviest criterion, and the brief does not
yet say who the recommendation is for. A health-system CFO deciding whether to acquire the next
practice, a state attorney general or the FTC reviewing consolidation, a self-insured employer
choosing a network — each would read the same finding and do something different with it. Pick the
one you would most want to persuade, and write that into the brief.

One smaller thing while you are in there. "Evidence that integration lowers healthcare costs or
prices" would fire on any single study anyone can cite. Which price, and how large a difference
between integrated and independent practice would count as lowering it? Give each falsification
condition a measure and a rough size now, before you read the studies, so the literature does not
end up deciding for you.

**In order:**

1. Name the one relationship and the two sources — employment share by year, price or price
   differential — in the brief, and sketch the figure from whatever the two series allow.
2. Cut the concept list to the two your hypothesis uses.
3. Name the decision-maker.
4. Give each falsification condition a measure and a threshold.
