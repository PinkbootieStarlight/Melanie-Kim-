# Physician Integration and Its Impact on Healthcare Delivery and Healthcare Costs

## The Challenge

More physicians are moving away from independent practices toward employment under larger organizations such as hospitals, other healthcare organizations, and private equity. Identified factors propelling this change include reimbursement transformation, increased administrative burden, rising costs of malpractice/liability protection, and compliance concerns.

## My Hypothesis

Physician integration may generate genuine economies of scale and administrative efficiencies, but increased concentration and provider bargaining power may contribute to higher healthcare prices. The analysis will determine whether measurable efficiencies are sufficient to offset those effects.

## Microeconomics

The two primary economic concepts in this analysis are economies of scale and market power. Physician integration may create efficiencies through shared administrative resources, access to capital, and reduced transaction costs. At the same time, integration may increase market concentration and provider bargaining power. Other effects, including referral patterns, site of care, barriers to entry, and competition, will be considered as mechanisms through which these two economic forces may affect healthcare prices and spending.

## Macroeconomics

Physician integration may contribute to structural changes in the healthcare labor market and affect aggregate healthcare spending.

## Planned Analysis

My analysis will synthesize evidence from peer-reviewed scientific and economic literature, healthcare policy research, and national physician-practice data.

* The primary measurable relationship will be physician integration and healthcare prices. AMA physician-practice benchmark data will document the shift from physician-owned private practice toward employment and hospital integration. Cooper et al. will provide the primary empirical evidence on the relationship between hospital acquisition of physician practices and changes in physician prices.
* The analysis will examine whether economies of scale and organizational efficiencies translate into lower healthcare prices or spending, rather than assuming that greater organizational efficiency results in lower costs for patients or payers.
* Market power will be evaluated through changes in market concentration and provider bargaining power. Referral patterns and site of care will be examined as additional mechanisms through which integration may affect healthcare prices and spending.
* Evidence on quality and patient outcomes will be used to determine whether higher prices or spending are accompanied by meaningful improvements in patient care.
* The findings will be considered from the perspective of federal and state antitrust regulators, including the Federal Trade Commission, when evaluating physician-practice acquisitions and healthcare consolidation.
  
## What Could Prove the Argument Wrong?
* The price component of the hypothesis would be challenged by strong and consistent evidence that physician integration results in price reductions that are both statistically significant and economically meaningful compared with similar independent practices. The size of the decrease will be considered in relation to the price effects reported in the physician-integration literature.
* The market-power component would be challenged if the evidence shows both no economically meaningful increase in physician-market concentration and no meaningful increase in provider bargaining power with payers following integration.
* Integration may create real organizational efficiencies, but greater organizational efficiency does not necessarily make healthcare less expensive. For these efficiencies to offset higher healthcare prices or spending, they should translate into measurable savings for patients or payers that are at least large enough to offset the additional spending associated with integration.
* Quality will count as an offset only if the evidence demonstrates improvements in patient outcomes that are both statistically significant and clinically meaningful. Improvements in administrative coordination, physician throughput, or other process measures alone would not be enough.
